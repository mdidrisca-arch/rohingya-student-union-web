When Education Has No Clear Future: The Rohingya Education Crisis in the Camps

By Rohingya Student Union · October 22, 2026 · 6 min read

There is a question children are often asked as they grow up:

“What do you want to be when you grow up?”

Some dream of becoming doctors, teachers, engineers or lawyers. Like children everywhere, Rohingya children have ambitions for their lives.

But for many growing up in the refugee camps of Cox’s Bazar, a harder question follows:

“How will you get there?”

For thousands of Rohingya students, the path from the classroom to higher education and professional life remains uncertain. They may study for years without knowing whether they can continue their education, obtain recognized qualifications or pursue the careers they dream of.

We often measure the Rohingya education crisis through enrolment figures, the number of learning centres and available humanitarian funding. These measures matter, but they do not tell us whether students can progress beyond the classroom.

The challenge is not simply getting Rohingya children into school. It is giving them a realistic path forward.

Access to education is only the beginning

Since the mass displacement of 2017, more than a million Rohingya refugees have sought safety in Bangladesh. Many children have spent most of their lives in the camps, where educational opportunities remain limited.

Most Rohingya children have relied on humanitarian learning centres and community-based schools, with restricted access to Bangladesh's formal education system. The introduction of the Myanmar Curriculum has been an important step, but curriculum access alone does not guarantee a complete educational pathway.

Students need opportunities to progress to higher grades, sit recognized examinations, obtain qualifications and pursue further studies.

For many, these opportunities remain unclear, particularly as they reach adolescence. Some leave school because of limited educational options, while girls may face additional barriers related to safety, household responsibilities and family expectations.

When students cannot see a next step, years of learning risk becoming disconnected from their future ambitions.

The question is straightforward: What happens when a child wants to continue studying, but the education system offers nowhere to go?

What comes after Grade 8, 9 or 10?

For many Rohingya students, the transition to higher levels of education is one of the greatest challenges.

Can they continue to higher secondary education? Can they sit for recognized examinations? Can they apply to universities or study medicine, engineering, computer science and other professional subjects?

The answers are often uncertain, and opportunities may depend on individual circumstances rather than an accessible system.

Some Rohingya students have overcome significant barriers to continue their education. Their achievements deserve recognition, but individual success cannot substitute for reliable educational pathways.

Students should not have to depend on exceptional circumstances to pursue further studies.

A functioning education system must offer clear routes from one level to the next, allowing students to plan their education with confidence.

Funding cuts put education at risk

Education in the camps depends heavily on humanitarian funding. When resources decline, learning centres may close, teachers may lose their livelihoods and students may lose access to classes.

In 2025, UNICEF warned that education funding cuts could affect around 230,000 Rohingya children.

For students, these disruptions mean interrupted lessons, reduced support and uncertainty about whether their education will continue. A closure can undo months of progress and make returning to school more difficult.

Education cannot depend entirely on short-term projects. Children need consistent teaching, learning materials and sustained support throughout their school years.

Funding decisions should therefore consider not only how many children can enrol today, but also whether they can complete their education tomorrow.

Rohingya educators must be part of the solution

Despite these challenges, Rohingya teachers and community educators continue to support children's learning, often with limited resources.

Their understanding of the community's language, culture and experiences helps them connect with students and create more inclusive learning environments. With appropriate training, fair compensation and professional development, they can make an even greater contribution.

The Rohingya Student Union has been involved in initiatives to strengthen education and youth capacity in the camps. Its teacher development activities focus on improving teaching practices, supporting inclusive classrooms and strengthening safeguarding.

Such efforts demonstrate the importance of community-led education. Rohingya people should have a meaningful role in shaping the programs that affect their children.

Young people, too, should be recognized as potential educators, mentors, researchers and leaders—not simply recipients of assistance.

Girls deserve equal opportunities to learn

Rohingya girls can face additional barriers as they grow older, including domestic responsibilities, safety concerns, family expectations and limited access to suitable learning spaces.

Addressing these challenges requires safe classrooms, supportive families and communities, and practical measures that help girls remain in school.

Girls' education must be central to any serious response to the crisis. Every girl deserves the opportunity to develop her abilities, pursue further studies and make decisions about her future.

Her education matters in its own right, as well as for the knowledge, skills and perspectives she can contribute to her community.

Education is about more than employment

Education helps people understand their rights, evaluate information, express their views and participate in decisions that affect their lives.

For a displaced and stateless community, these opportunities carry particular importance. The Rohingya community needs doctors, teachers, engineers, lawyers, journalists, researchers and social workers. It also needs educated young people who can preserve their history and represent their community's experiences.

Rohingya children should not have to choose between pursuing an education and maintaining their cultural identity. They deserve the knowledge and skills to engage with the wider world while remaining connected to their community.

Building a pathway beyond primary education

Addressing the crisis requires a long-term approach that supports students from early learning through secondary and higher education.

Key priorities include:

  • Expanding secondary education so adolescents can continue studying.
  • Establishing recognized examination and certification pathways that support further education.
  • Improving access to universities through scholarships, partnerships and appropriate distance-learning opportunities.
  • Strengthening digital and vocational education without replacing academic pathways.
  • Supporting Rohingya teachers through training, fair compensation and sustained resources.
  • Removing barriers for girls through safer, more accessible learning environments.
  • Securing long-term funding to prevent repeated interruptions.

These priorities require cooperation among Rohingya communities, humanitarian organizations, education partners, donors and relevant authorities.

Progress must also be measured beyond enrolment. Attendance, learning outcomes, grade progression, completion rates and access to further education are equally important indicators.

The central question should be: How many Rohingya children can continue their education and use what they learn to pursue their goals?

A responsibility we cannot postpone

Rohingya students, teachers and community organizations are already working to strengthen education under difficult circumstances. Their efforts deserve sustained support, not an expectation that they will overcome every structural barrier alone.

Humanitarian assistance—including food, shelter, healthcare and protection—remains essential. Education also requires reliable funding and long-term planning that extends beyond immediate emergency needs.

Children cannot pause their development until funding improves or a durable solution to displacement is found. Every year of interrupted learning can narrow their options and make further progress more difficult.

The question is no longer whether Rohingya children deserve an education. It is whether the systems supporting them will provide the continuity, qualifications and opportunities necessary to make that education count.

A classroom is an essential starting point, but it cannot carry the entire responsibility.

Rohingya children deserve an education system that allows them to progress, gain recognized qualifications and pursue their ambitions. Building that system requires sustained investment, stronger educational pathways and meaningful Rohingya participation in decisions about their future.

Education should not end where opportunity ends. It should open doors to opportunities that Rohingya children have long been denied.

For a generation growing up in exile, building that future is not a distant concern. It is a responsibility we must address today.
